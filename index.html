04 / UN—SCALABLE

You met this one at the door.

The brand is built on being hard to get. The cap table is built on getting big.

One of those has to give, and it will not be the cap table. Two funds just made their first fragrance bet on you, and both of them said the quiet part out loud: culture as much as commerce, a brand you want to be seen with, priced like it respects itself.

The failure mode is well documented and it is not dramatic. It is gradual. More SKUs, wider availability, one exception that made sense at the time — and a premium anti-dupe position quietly becomes another dupe brand. Bella Vita did ₹456 Cr last year on ₹90 Cr of advertising for a 4.61% margin. That is what the bottom of this market actually pays.

The other direction has a cliff too, and that evidence is unusually clean. VF bought Supreme for $2.2bn and sold it four years later for $1.5bn — revenue fell across the hold, and the diagnosis was that opening stores removed a layer of scarcity. In India the gap is starker: the purest drop brands here are ₹4–30 Cr businesses. Snitch, which won on newness velocity instead, did ₹900 Cr.

Scarcity alone doesn't get you there. Volume alone costs you the brand.

→Scarcity has to stop being a mood and become a mechanic. Hold the unit scarce; scale the frequency and the tiers around it.

You are not selling perfume. You are selling a collectible.

The category you invented is an accessory — worn, clipped, carried, seen. The sub-brand on the box is DANGLE. The debut isn't a hero scent with flankers; it is four names from one grammar, which is the structure of a set. DIS—LOYAL is photographed in a clear acrylic case like evidence. This matters more than it sounds. Perfume repeats on depletion — slow, unpredictable, outside your control. Collectibles repeat on set completion, where the unit of demand is "which one don't I have" — a question you author entirely. It is also the one category on earth where scarcity is the growth engine rather than the ceiling on it. Pop Mart and SNKRS both grow by raising drop frequency and access tiers while holding unit scarcity constant — the opposite of widening distribution, which is the move that cost Supreme its premium.

The sting: you shipped the entire set in one drop, un-serialised, with no ownership record. Nobody at CUNIN can answer "who owns two of the four" — which is the highest-converting message in a set-based business.
